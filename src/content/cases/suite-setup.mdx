## Context

Denti.AI builds AI tools for dental clinics: Voice Perio for hands-free periodontal
charting, Imaging for X-ray analysis, and Scribe for clinical notes. Clinics usually get
them together as OneStop Clinical, a small AI operating system for the practice.

The products were built by different teams at different times. Each came with its own
installer and its own settings, organized by its own logic.

## The problem

A clinic buys one suite but gets several separate products, each with its own installer
and settings. A Denti.AI onboarding specialist configured them across tray-menu windows,
tabs, nested dialogs and a separate web page. The microphone alone could be chosen in
three places, and window names often didn't match their contents: the switch for the
entire Scribe product sat under Advanced.

The tech lead set the goal: **one installer for the whole suite and one place to configure
everything in it**.

## Audit

I started by collecting every setting from every product: **80+ controls across at least
six windows**, on two platforms. Then I marked up what each one actually belonged to,
regardless of where it happened to live.

<Figure src="/media/suite-setup/audit" caption="The original settings windows, marked up by what each setting belongs to" />

The markup showed a structure the products never had. Settings fell into clear groups: the
connection to the clinic's PMS, account and activation, audio devices, connection and
updates, maintenance and sync, and the behavior of each product. Duplicates collapsed into
one entry each. Settings that no longer did anything were cut.

## One rule for every level

The harder question was not where each setting goes, but how the whole structure behaves.
I borrowed the answer from operating system settings: **a setting looks and works the same
whether it sits on the first screen or three levels deep**. People learn the pattern once,
and it holds as sections grow.

That principle mattered more than any single screen. When the team later moved items
between sections, nothing in the logic had to change. Imaging was deliberately left out of
the first pass: the developers will add its settings themselves, following the same rules.

## Installer

Instead of a separate installer per product, the clinic now runs one. It installs the whole
suite or only the selected products, and connects the clinic's PMS during setup.

The engineers' draft had a reasonable number of steps. The problem was what was inside
them. The PMS and the install path were asked on two different screens, though each is
needed only once. Every screen also carried a Back link that served no purpose: it was
needed neither to return to a previous step nor to leave the installation. I removed both,
so **each screen asks for one thing, once**.

## Process and outcome

I iterated with the tech lead, then presented the final version to the whole engineering
team and worked their feedback in. The design was approved and taken into development.

- One installer for the whole suite instead of one per product
- One settings app, desktop with a web counterpart, instead of 6+ windows and a separate web page
- Every setting in exactly one place
- A principle the team can extend without a designer, starting with Imaging

Today setup is done by Denti.AI's onboarding specialists. The new installer and settings
are designed so that clinic staff could do it themselves.
